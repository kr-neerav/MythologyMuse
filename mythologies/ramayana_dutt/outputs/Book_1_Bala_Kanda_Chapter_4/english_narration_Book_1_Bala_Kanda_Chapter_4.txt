Previously we saw how Maharishi Valmiki asked Narada who the most perfect and righteous man is. Narada told him the brief story of Shri Ram and returned to his own realm through the sky. Now Valmiki sits alone on a seat of kusha grass, meditating deeply on Ram's character through yoga. From that meditation rises our story today, at the time when Shri Ram had already received the kingdom of Ayodhya.
After Ram received the kingdom, the revered sage Valmiki composed his complete history in beautiful verses. It held twenty-four thousand shlokas, five hundred cantos and seven kandas including the Uttara. Every word was filled with deep meaning. With his yogic vision he joined past events with things yet to happen. When the work was complete, he began to wonder who should first present this great story before assemblies.
While he was thinking this, Kush and Lav arrived in the dress of ascetics and touched his feet. Both were radiant princes, yet they lived in the hermitage and knew dharma deeply. Their voices had a sweet ring and they grasped the meaning of poetry very quickly. Their memory was very sharp and they were advanced in Vedic study. Seeing this, Valmiki first taught them how to understand the meaning of the Vedas.
Then the vow-observing Valmiki taught them the entire Ramayana by heart. This tale centered on the life of Sita and the destruction of Ravana, the descendant of Pulastya. Both brothers were beautiful like Gandharvas and skilled in music and dance. They knew the rules of melody like Sthana and Murchhana. They learned to sing in three measures and seven notes, with stringed instruments like the veena. That singing carried love, pathos, humour, anger, fear and heroism.
They also understood the rules of drama and their voices had a honey-like sweetness. Both blameless princes were born of Rama himself, and to see them was like seeing the reflection of the sun or moon in a mirror. With full devotion they memorized the entire sacred story. Then with concentrated minds they began to sing it in assemblies of ascetics, Brahmanas and good people, exactly as they had learned it.
One day those pure and noble brothers sang this poem in an assembly of pure-souled ascetics. Every auspicious mark shone on their bodies. Hearing the music, all the ascetics were filled with wonder, and with tears in their eyes they cried, well done, well done. The saints who cherished duty praised them joyfully. They said, what enchanting music, what sweetness in the verses. All this happened long ago, yet it feels as if we are seeing it before our eyes.
Both brothers kept singing together in one tune, in a high pitch with pure notes like Shadja, and spellbound the listeners. Even the great sages proud of their penance never tired of praising them. Pleased, someone in the assembly gave them a water pitcher, someone gave a garment made of tree bark. Someone gave a black deer skin, someone a sacred thread, and someone a clay or wooden kamandalu used by ascetics.
Then gifts began to pour in. One great soul gave a girdle of munja grass, another gave a mat to sit on, and another a small kaupina cloth. Someone gave an axe, someone a red cloth, and someone a thread to tie their matted locks. Someone gave twine for binding firewood, someone a vessel for offerings, someone sacred firewood, and someone a seat made of fig wood. Some cried Swasti, some blessed them with long life.
All the truth-speaking ascetics blessed them and said, wonderful is this story. Skilled in music, you two have sung beautifully to the end this poem that delights ear and heart and grants long life and prosperity. This same poem will give poets themes for the future. Honored everywhere, one day those two singers were seen in a street of Ayodhya, lined here and there with stalls.
There Bharata's elder brother Shri Ram saw them and called them to his house. Rama, the destroyer of enemies, welcomed those worthy ones with full respect. Then he sat on a beautiful golden throne among his brothers and ministers. Looking at the handsome and modest brothers before him, he told Lakshmana, Bharata and Shatrughna to listen to the story of excellent meaning and meter sung by these divinely inspired ones.
At Rama's order both began to sing. Their sweet and clear voices rose in a high pitch like the strain of a veena. The listeners felt their hair stand on end and their minds and hearts blossomed. The ears of the whole assembly were captured by that song. Then Rama said, though Kush and Lav look like ascetics of hard penance, the marks of royalty shine clearly on them. This story spreads my fame, so listen to it knowing its great worth.
Commanded by Rama, they began to sing in the Marga style. Seated in the middle of the court, Rama was completely drawn into that music. He wished his history to live forever and reach every generation. Thus the Ramayana composed by Valmiki had now travelled from the teacher's hermitage to the king's court, where Shri Ram himself sat to hear the tale of his own life.
Question: Why did Valmiki first awaken Kush-Lav to the meaning of the Vedas instead of directly handing them the sweet Rama story? Reflection: In my view this sequence itself is Valmiki's greatest revolution. He knew that without discernment a story becomes mere entertainment, not instruction. Gandharvas too have sweet throats and firm memory, but only a cultivated intellect can weigh meaning. So he first gave the eye of interpretation, then the lamp of narrative. Today we do the reverse — we first make a viral voice, then think of teaching values, and that is why talent loses its way. To sing a story is art, to live a story is shastra. Takeaway: This week before forwarding any inspiring story or reel, pause a moment and ask yourself what its dharmic meaning is, and teach one young person not the story but how to read its meaning.
Question: Why did Valmiki not go himself to Ayodhya to present so great a history, why did he make two children its medium? Reflection: I see in this a deep understanding of the psychology of power. A king may protect a preaching sage, but he will not listen to his tale; power bows only before an innocent voice. Kush-Lav are called Rama's own reflections, like sun and moon in a mirror. A father cannot see himself until his sons sing him back to himself. By stepping aside Valmiki made the tale larger than its author, moving it from his lap to living throats, so it would not remain one generation's memory but become the breath of ages. Takeaway: This week do not publicize one of your principles or works yourself, entrust it to a young person or student to say in their own language, and simply sit as a listener.
Question: Why were even ascetics of hard penance filled with tears on hearing the tale, why was history bound in seven notes and rasas? Reflection: For me the answer is this, intellect forgets, the heart does not. Valmiki knew dry facts across five hundred cantos would hold no one. So he wove love, pathos, humour and heroism into melody, rhythm and enactment, so listeners would not merely hear but see. That is why the sages said, it happened long ago, yet we see it before our eyes. This is not a miracle of beauty but a science of memory — only when knowledge becomes tears does it become samskara. Detachment does not mean absence of feeling, but purification of feeling. Takeaway: This week turn one dry household rule or lesson into a feeling-filled story, song or enactment, and notice which words truly stay in the heart.
Question: Why did the all-knowing sages give the singers no gold coins, but simple gifts like a pitcher, an axe, firewood and a kaupina? Reflection: I see in this the most original definition of giving. The forest sages had no royal treasury, but they had understanding of life. They weighed praise not by price but by usefulness — they gave not to decorate the singer but to sustain his sadhana. The axe will serve his ascetic work, the pitcher will quench thirst, the seat will steady practice. And the greatest gift came at the end, the blessing that this poem will give themes to future poets. That is, true reward is not present price but future fertility. Takeaway: This week give an emerging talent not just a like or applause, but a practical object, time or contact that sustains their practice.
Question: Why did Rama, himself the embodiment of propriety, sit in court to hear his own tale, and is calling it fame-enhancing not pride? Reflection: This episode seems to me not pride but self-vision. Until a hero hears his tale from another mouth, he remains only a doer, never a seer. Sitting on the throne Rama turned from king to listener, and he recognized royal marks within ascetic dress — that is, learning to see not by outer form but by essence. And the wish to keep history alive is not self-publicity but responsibility, so future citizens may judge kings on the touchstone of this tale. Only one who learns to listen becomes worthy to govern. Takeaway: This week listen for five minutes to yourself through a trusted person without interrupting, offer no justification, only ask what does my story teach you.
