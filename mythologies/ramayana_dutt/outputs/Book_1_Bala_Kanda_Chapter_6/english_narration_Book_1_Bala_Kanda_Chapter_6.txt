Previously we saw how Maharshi Valmiki asked Narada about the perfectly righteous man, Narada narrated Rama's brief tale and returned to his own realm, and Valmiki, absorbed in meditation, composed the complete Ramayana in twenty-four thousand verses. From today we will hear that same great Ramayana tale from the very beginning, from the Ikshvaku dynasty that begins with Prajapati and whose foundation was laid by Ikshvaku.
In that great city named Ayodhya lived King Dasharatha, foremost among great warriors of the Ikshvaku line, knower of the Vedas, commanding every resource, far-sighted and highly valiant, and deeply dear to all people of villages and towns. He performed sacrifices, remained devoted to his duties and was self-controlled like a Maharshi, and was famed in the three worlds as a righteous royal sage.
He was full of strength, destroyer of foes, blessed with many friends, with senses fully under control. In accumulated wealth and splendour he was equal to Indra and Kubera, and just as Manu protected his subjects, he protected his people. Just as Indra rules Amaravati, that king, firm in his promise, ruled the excellent city of Ayodhya while upholding dharma, artha and kama.
In that excellent city people were happy and righteous-souled, highly learned, each content with his own possessions, free from greed and always speaking the truth. There was no one who lacked a plentiful supply of good things, and there was no householder who did not possess horses and cows, grain and wealth in abundant measure.
Nowhere in all Ayodhya could one see a person sunk in lust, unsightly, crooked-minded, unlettered or atheistic. All women and men were of excellent character, with senses restrained and minds cheerful. In both their work and conduct they were spotless like Maharshis, living a pure life and observing noble decorum.
All people wore earrings in their ears, crowns on their heads and garlands around their necks, and richly enjoyed the finest things of life. They stayed clean through bathing, anointed their limbs with sandal paste, remained fragrant with perfumes, ate pure food and gave generously in charity. Wearing armlets, Nishka necklaces and bracelets on their hands, they kept their desires under control.
In Ayodhya there was no house where the sacred Agnihotra fire was not lit, no person who did not perform sacrifices with due rites, and no man of mean thinking. There was no thief, no one engaged in blameworthy or improper livelihood, and no person born of low birth of impure descent.
The self-controlled Brahmanas there always remained devoted to performing their own duties, gave in charity, studied the scriptures and accepted gifts only with discernment. Among them there was no atheist, no liar, no poorly learned, slandering, incompetent or illiterate person. Every Brahmana was a knower of the Vedas and Vedangas, observed vows, gave gifts by the thousands, and none was cowardly, deranged in mind or afflicted.
In Ayodhya no man or woman was ever seen who lacked splendour and lustre, who was devoid of beauty, or who had no reverence for their monarch in the heart. Every person appeared handsome, attractive and courteous, every face glowed with cheerfulness and all people honoured the king from their hearts.
The people of all four orders, who honoured Brahmanas as foremost, served gods and guests, were full of gratitude, generous givers, brave and mighty. They honoured their gods, welcomed arriving guests and protected dharma with their valour, so that a beautiful union of sacrifice and heroism was seen throughout the city.
In that most excellent city people attained long life and lived always fully observing dharma and truth. They always lived in full households together with their sons, grandsons and wives. Lineages flourished in every home, bonds held deep and unbroken affection, and because of the righteous life, happiness and contentment pervaded every family.
Kshatriyas obeyed the Brahmanas, Vaishyas followed the Kshatriyas, and Shudras, engaged in their proper occupations, served the three other orders. Thus all four orders stood firm in their own duties. That great lord of the Ikshvaku line governed that vast city with exceeding skill and order, just as the wise Manu had governed it before him.
Just as a mountain cave is filled with lions, that city was filled with warriors radiant like blazing fire. They walked the straight path, tolerated no foe and were accomplished in the science of arms. In the city there were excellent horses in abundance, born in Kamboja, Bahlika, Vanayu and on the banks of the Sindhu, esteemed as fine as Hari's own best horse.
In the city there were fearsome elephants born on the Vindhya and Himalaya, rutting, exceedingly strong and huge like mountains. There were Bhadra, Mandra and Mriga breeds of elephants, with compact limbs, heavy or tall bodies. There were also superior elephants born from the union of all three and from the unions of Bhadra-Mandra, Bhadra-Mriga and Mriga-Mandra. These elephants of Mahapadma, Anjana and Vamana lines were excellent like Airavata and looked like hills.
That city extended over two yojanas and truly justified its name Ayodhya, meaning that which cannot be conquered. Repressing his enemies, the great and exceedingly powerful King Dasharatha ruled over it as the moon presides over the stars. Like Indra, that lord of the earth ruled a city with strong gates and bolts, auspicious, adorned with beautiful palaces and crowded with thousands of people.
Question: Why does Valmiki describe the city of Ayodhya in such detail before introducing Rama himself — why the city before the hero? Reflection: In my view this is the most radical signal that Rama is not a miracle dropped from the sky but the product of a healthy ecology. Today we hunt for great individuals yet forget great environments. Here the king is true to his word, so the subjects speak truth; the king is self-controlled, so the city is free from lust — the character of leadership becomes the character of the collective. Ayodhya teaches that dharma is first a system, then a person. Takeaway: This week audit your small Ayodhya — your home, team or circle of friends — and enforce one rule there that you will follow first yourself.
Question: Why is Dasharatha called in the same breath splendid like Indra-Kubera and self-controlled like a Maharshi — can enjoyment and restraint coexist? Reflection: This is the most misunderstood riddle of Indian dharma. We treat enjoyment as the opposite of renunciation, but Valmiki says the true king is one who holds everything yet is enslaved by nothing. Dasharatha upholds dharma, artha and kama together — wealth and desire bound within dharma, so earrings and garlands, sandal and perfume become discipline, not lust. Power protects only when tied by restraint, otherwise it exploits. Takeaway: From tomorrow pick one pleasure — food, phone or shopping — and for seven days enjoy it only within a limit you set in advance, as a master, not as a slave.
Question: A city where no one is greedy, no thief, no liar — is this poetic exaggeration or a psychological map? Reflection: I feel Ayodhya describes the inner more than the outer. Ayodhya itself means that which cannot be conquered by war, and the mind becomes unconquerable only when it rests in contentment. Greed is the breach through which theft, lies and lust enter; where everyone is satisfied with their share, crime finds no place to be born. So Valmiki does not count sins, he cuts their root — discontent. The modern city increases police, Ayodhya increases contentment. Takeaway: This week close one door of greed — comparison on social media or mindless shopping scrolls — keep it shut for half an hour every day, and see how invincible the mind feels.
Question: Kshatriyas under Brahmanas, Vaishyas behind Kshatriyas — how should we read this varna discipline with today's sense of equality; is it mere praise of old hierarchy? Reflection: I see here role-clarity more than hierarchy. In Ayodhya each order is defined by service to the other, not by exploitation — the Brahmana gives discernment, the Kshatriya gives protection, the Vaishya gives nourishment, the Shudra connects through service. The irony is that today, while claiming to be all equal, we compete with each other in every task, so no one stays firm in their own dharma. Valmiki's ideal is not high-low but mutual gratitude, which is why all are called servers of gods and guests. Takeaway: On paper write three roles of your life — such as parent, colleague, citizen — and fix one duty for each role that you will perform this week without complaint.
Question: Why so much adornment in the city of dharma — sandal-perfume, earrings-garlands and rutting elephants-horses; does the path to liberation not go through simplicity? Reflection: Valmiki surprises us — here restraint is not gloom, it is beauty. Bathing, fragrance, pure food and charity appear in the same line, meaning adorning the body is also sadhana when done from decorum, not from lust. And Ayodhya is by name unconquerable, yet filled with lion-like warriors and mountain-like elephants — because compassion needs strength to endure. Peace without strength gets looted, dharma without beauty turns dry. Takeaway: This week choose one beautiful discipline — sitting quietly for two minutes after bathing, decorating one corner of the house, or eating food with reverence — and live dharma by making it beautiful.
