Previously, we saw how Maharishi Valmiki asked Devarshi Narada who is that perfect man in this world, full of dharma, truth, compassion and valour. Narada was pleased and promised to tell him the story of that noble character. Now, after Narada's blessings, the tale moves forward, where a tiny incident is about to give birth to a great epic.
After listening to Narada, Valmiki worshipped him duly along with his disciples. Honoured, Narada took leave and left through the sky to his own realm. After he left, Valmiki reached the banks of the Tamasa river flowing near the Ganga. Seeing a holy and beautiful spot free of mud, he told his disciple Bharadwaja, child, put down the pitcher and give me my bark cloth for bathing.
Obeying his guru, Bharadwaja at once brought the bark cloth. Before bathing, Valmiki strolled in the forest and looked around. Then he saw a pair of krauncha birds near a tree, singing sweetly in joyful play. At that very moment a cruel fowler aimed without any enmity and killed the male bird. Drenched in blood, he writhed in pain, and his mate began to scream and weep in grief.
Seeing the struggling bird and the weeping female, Valmiki's heart filled with compassion. Judging it a grave injustice, drowned in sorrow he cursed the fowler, O Nishada, you have killed one of a pair lost in love, so you shall never find honour. As soon as he spoke he was startled, what has come from my mouth. Then he told his disciple, look, this sentence is bound in equal words and rhythm, it can be sung to the veena, born of my sorrow it shall be called a shloka.
Disciple Bharadwaja happily supported his guru's words, which gave Valmiki satisfaction. Then he bathed at that holy bank and returned to the hermitage thinking about the verse. Bharadwaja followed behind him carrying the water-filled pitcher on his back. At the hermitage Valmiki spoke of this and that on the surface, but the same metre kept echoing in his mind. Then, to everyone's amazement, the radiant four-faced Brahma himself arrived to meet him.
The moment he saw Brahma, Valmiki rose at once in wonder and stood silently before him with folded hands. He bowed low, offered water to wash his feet, gave him a seat and worshipped him. Seated on the honoured seat, Brahma asked after Valmiki's welfare and asked him also to sit. Even after sitting, Valmiki's mind stayed fixed in that sorrow, he kept thinking what a great sin the fowler had committed by killing for no reason the sweet-singing bird.
Lost in grief, Valmiki kept repeating the same verse in his mind and mourned for the female kraunchi. Seeing this, Brahma smiled and said, this metre from your lips will become immortal, have no doubt about it. By my own will this speech has burst forth. Now compose in this same metre the complete story of Rama. All that you heard from Narada and did not hear, about Rama, Lakshmana, Videha's daughter Sita and the rakshasas, even what Narada does not know will be revealed to you, and not one word of yours will be false.
As long as mountains and oceans remain on earth, your Ramayana will spread among people. And as long as this story is sung, you will dwell both in my world and in this world. Saying this, Brahma vanished on the spot, and Valmiki stood amazed with his disciples. The disciples began to sing that same shloka again and again, with its equal syllables, four parts and deep pathos. They said, now our guru will compose the whole Ramayana in this sweet metre.
Thus blessed, the graceful and greatly famed ascetic Valmiki bound the tale of Rama's dynasty in hundreds of sweet shlokas. Listen, the life story of Rama, noblest of the Raghu clan, and the tale of the end of ten-headed Ravana, composed by the sage in simple, melodious and rhythmic words. This is where the Ramayana is born.
Question: Why does the great epic begin not with a blessing but with a curse, and why is compassion first heard in the voice of anger? Reflection: I feel Valmiki shows us that shoka and shloka share one root — when sorrow overflows the heart, it bursts out as rhythm. His rage at the fowler is not really justice, it is the soul's cry at the loss of love; anger is only compassion boiling over. Psychology says the same — formless pain becomes trauma, but rhythmic pain becomes meaning, and so lament bound in metre turned immortal. That is why the Ramayana starts not with war but with separation. Takeaway: This week when sharp grief or anger rises inside, do not hurl it at someone, give it rhythm in four lines — that will be your first shloka.
Question: The fowler was only doing his daily work, so why did Valmiki treat a killing without enmity as such grave adharma? Reflection: For me the key lies in that small qualifier — without cause, without hatred. Valmiki does not oppose eating meat, he opposes shattering love absorbed in play. The male krauncha did not die in battle, he was killed while singing and loving — violence aimed at life's celebration itself. That is the shape of cruelty today, when out of boredom, views or profit we loose arrows into someone's play, trust or relationship. The sage's curse is not punishment but mirror — you stole a pair's joy, how will you earn glory? Takeaway: This week pause once and notice where you become the Nishada — mocking laughter, interrupting in a meeting, or trolling — and choose silence instead of shooting the arrow.
Question: Why does the wise Valmiki startle at his own utterance and ask, what has come from my mouth? Reflection: This moment feels most human to me, because true creation happens first and understanding follows. Valmiki did not construct the metre, it flowed from grief — equal feet, equal syllables, a rhythm fit for the veena settled on its own. This is not grammatical skill but compassion's natural music, what we today call flow, when the unconscious outruns the conscious. That is why he at once makes the disciple a witness — look, hold this — because the first spark needs a second pair of eyes to survive. Takeaway: This week when in anger, sorrow or love an unusual true line escapes your lips, do not let it fly away, write it down at once and confirm it by sharing with someone you trust.
Question: Why just before the killing is Tamasa's mud-free pure ghat compared to the mind of good men? Reflection: In my view this simile is not decoration but condition. Reflection does not appear in muddy water, just so another's pain does not appear to a mind filled with noise and self-interest. Valmiki sought purity for bathing, but nature gave him purity of seeing — only then he heard both the joy of playing birds and the scream of the female. In city crowds we witness such killings daily, yet with mud inside we remain unmoved. Sensitivity is cleanliness before morality. Takeaway: This week choose ten screen-free minutes daily in a quiet place — balcony, park, temple — let the mind's mud settle, then see whose pain appears clearly to you for the first time.
Question: Why does Brahma snatch Valmiki's private grief and order him to make it the vast tale of Rama, Sita and the rakshasas? Reflection: I think Brahma here converts wound into work. Lonely sorrow stays lament, but acknowledged sorrow becomes resolve — your pain is not only for this kraunchi but for every separated Sita. Hence he says, by my will this burst forth, now make it Ramayana; suffering was divine design, not punishment. And he grants two boons — even the unknown will be seen and not one word will be false — because a story seen through compassion is a larger truth than fact, it outlasts mountains. Takeaway: This week name one old hurt and decide whom it will serve — call that person, write for them or teach them, turn wound into blessing.
