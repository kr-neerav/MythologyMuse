Previously, we saw how Maharshi Valmiki asked Narada who is the most complete and righteous man in this world. Narada promised to tell him the story of Shri Ram. After narrating it and accepting Valmiki's worship, Narada returned to his own realm through the sky, and now Valmiki sits alone in deep thought where a small incident is about to awaken a great resolve.
After Narada departed, Valmiki sat facing the east on a mat of kusha grass. He sipped water as prescribed and fixed his mind on Shri Ram's character through yoga. By the power of yoga, everything appeared living before his eyes. Rama, Lakshmana, Sita, and King Dasharatha with his wives were clearly seen laughing, talking, and moving around.
Just as an amla fruit kept on the palm is seen clearly, Valmiki clearly saw Rama's past, present, and future. He came to know all the hardships endured in the forest by Rama, firm in his vow, along with his wife Sita and Lakshmana. After this true vision, he resolved to write Rama's story. Following what Narada had told, he composed the history of the Raghu dynasty, deep like the ocean, sweet to the ears, and granting dharma, artha, kama, and moksha.
First, Valmiki saw Rama's birth, his strength, compassion for all, popularity, patience, truthfulness, and goodness. Then his journey with Vishwamitra, their wonderful dialogue, and the protection of the sacrifice. After that, the breaking of Shiva's bow in Janakpur and the celebration of marriage with Sita. And then on the way, the encounter with Parashurama, where Rama's calm courage and radiance shine forth. These virtues of Dasharatha's son become the foundation of the story.
Then comes a great turning point in the tale. Preparations are made in Ayodhya for Rama's coronation, but Kaikeyi's opposition stops everything. The consecration is obstructed and Rama receives fourteen years of exile. King Dasharatha laments in grief and gives up his life. The people weep in sorrow, but Rama consoles them and sends them back to Ayodhya. This farewell marks the end of Ayodhya's happy life.
On the forest path, Rama meets Nishadraj Guha, crosses the Ganga, and the charioteer Sumantra returns to Ayodhya with the empty chariot. Then, after staying at Sage Bharadwaja's hermitage, they reach Chitrakuta on his advice and live there building a leaf hut. Bharata comes to persuade him back, and Rama offers oblations for his father. Taking Rama's wooden sandals, Bharata lives in Nandigram and rules in his name.
From Chitrakuta, Rama goes to Dandaka forest, where the demon Viradha is slain. Then come meetings with sages Sharabhanga and Sutikshna. Sita meets Anasuya, who lovingly adorns her and blesses her. Sage Agastya gives Rama a divine bow. Then Shurpanakha arrives, and after her nose and ears are cut, Khara, Dushana, and Trishira attack, but Rama kills them. After this, Ravana plots Sita's abduction with Maricha.
Maricha takes the form of a golden deer, lures Rama and Lakshmana far away, and is killed. Meanwhile, Ravana abducts Sita. Rama laments and searches for her. On the way, the wounded vulture king Jatayu gives up his life. Then comes the encounter with the demon Kabandha and the sight of Lake Pampa. There Shabari lovingly feeds Rama berries and roots. On Pampa's banks, the grieving Rama meets Hanuman.
Hanuman introduces Rama to Sugriva on Mount Rishyamukha. Rama removes Sugriva's fear and forms a firm friendship with him. Then comes the fierce duel between Sugriva and Vali, in which Rama slays Vali. Tara's lament touches the heart, and Sugriva becomes king. Rama halts for the rainy season, and when the search for Sita does not begin in time, he burns with anger like a lion of the Raghu race. Then Sugriva gathers the monkey army.
Sugriva sends monkeys in all four directions and assigns a different region to each group. Rama gives Hanuman his ring as a token of identity. While searching, the monkeys with Jambavan reach a cave and then fast on the ocean shore, giving up food. There they meet Sampati. Then Hanuman climbs a mountain and leaps across the ocean. At the ocean's request, Mainaka offers him rest, and on the way he faces the shadow-catching demoness and Simhika.
At night, Hanuman enters Lanka and observes how one should conduct oneself in helpless times. He sees the tavern, the women's quarters, and the inner palaces, where Ravana and his Pushpaka chariot are also visible. Then in Ashoka Vatika he finds Sita. Hanuman gives her Rama's ring and delivers his message. The demonesses roar, when Trijata narrates her dream. Sita gives Hanuman her crest-jewel for trust.
After this, Hanuman uproots the trees of Ashoka Vatika. The demonesses flee and the guards are killed. The son of the wind god lets himself be captured and burns down Lanka with a terrible roar. Then he leaps back across the ocean and in Kishkindha forcefully enjoys the honey of Madhuvana. Hanuman consoles Rama and hands him Sita's jewel. This reunion fills Rama's sinking heart with new hope.
Then Rama asks the ocean for passage, and under Nala's leadership a bridge is built. The monkey army crosses the ocean and besieges Lanka by night. Vibhishana takes refuge with Rama and explains the means of victory. In the fierce battle, Kumbhakarna and Meghanada are killed. At last Ravana is slain and Sita is recovered in the enemy's city. Vibhishana is consecrated as king and the Pushpaka chariot appears. This victory of dharma touches every heart.
After the war, Rama returns toward Ayodhya in the Pushpaka and meets Sage Bharadwaja on the way. Hanuman is sent ahead to inform Bharata. Then comes the emotional reunion of Rama and Bharata, followed by Rama's coronation. All the monkey forces are bid farewell. Rama brings happiness to his subjects, but for the sake of the people he has to renounce Sita. Whatever further story of Rama on earth remains is told in the Uttara Kanda.
Question: Why did Valmiki sit facing the east and seek to see in yoga what he had already fully heard from Narada? Reflection: For me this is the difference between hearing and knowing. Narada gave words, but words are another's experience; yoga made them his own direct seeing. Bharata ruling from Nandigram on the authority of the sandals sounds like policy when heard, but seen in meditation it appears as living devotion to a brother. That is why seat, sipping water and facing east is not mere ritual but preparation of the creative mind — when the body is steady and the senses are calm, truth descends. Today we are surrounded by Naradas of information yet form opinions without pausing. Takeaway: This week do not react at once to a person or news; sit quietly for ten minutes, behold its human side in your mind, and only then speak or write.
Question: Why did supreme yogic vision show Valmiki not a supernatural light but humans laughing, talking and moving about? Reflection: This is the revolution of this chapter. We think truth will be found above, but Valmiki found truth in the courtyard — Dasharatha laughing and talking with his wives. Our mistake is to separate divinity from humanity; the Ramayana says godliness lies not in miracle but in sustaining relationships. When you see a great man laughing, you stop worshipping and start understanding. That is the psychology of compassion, which dissolves distance and awakens kinship. Takeaway: This week do not treat an ideal figure as an idol; observe closely one ordinary human moment — fatigue, laughter, worry — and learn by connecting with it.
Question: Does seeing past and future together like an amla on the palm prove that everything is predestined? Reflection: I think the reverse is true. Valmiki foresees Sita's abduction, yet Rama never looks like a puppet there; he laments, performs Jatayu's rites, and searches as far as Pampa. Total vision gives not fatalism but compassion. When you know the end, you stop blaming the character and start understanding him. Psychology calls this the witness perspective — seen from above, impulse lessens and responsibility grows. To know time is not to flee action but to give action meaning. Takeaway: This week when anxiety about the future haunts you, close your eyes, see your situation from afar as an amla on the palm, and ask — seen from the end, what is my right action now?
Question: To grant dharma, artha, kama and moksha, why did Valmiki compose ocean-like poetry sweet to ear and mind instead of dry instruction? Reflection: Because humans change not by command but by rasa. Shabari lovingly feeding berries to Rama at Pampa teaches no rule, yet it opens an entire scripture of devotion. The ocean simile is exact; the ocean has pearls and waves and depth; every reader finds according to his thirst. The modern mind too melts not by data but by story. That is why the Ramayana, though scripture, is song — first it seizes the ear, then the mind, then life. Takeaway: This week if you must teach someone a value, do not lecture; tell one true small story from your life in which that value was lived.
Question: Why did Valmiki retain the sting of Sita's renunciation after Lanka's victory and coronation instead of stopping at a happy ending? Reflection: This is Valmiki's honesty. He speaks in one breath of bringing happiness to the subjects and renouncing Sita — even the ideal king lives with a wound. Conventional thought says dharma gives happiness, but here dharma demands a price. Rama's care for the collective appears larger than private love, and that unsettles us. In modern life too behind every great success lies an invisible sacrifice we crop out of the poster. A complete story is one that counts the cost along with the victory. Takeaway: This week honestly write down one sacrifice or compromise hidden behind one of your successes and see whether it can still be repaired.
