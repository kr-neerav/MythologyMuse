The great sage Valmiki, devoted to austerity and Vedic study, asked Narada, the knower of words, who in this world at this time is filled with virtues and prowess. Who knows duty, is grateful, truthful, firm in vow, and seeks the welfare of all beings. Who is learned, knows how to keep subjects happy, is pleasing to behold, has his mind controlled, his anger restrained, is free from malice, and whom even the gods fear when enraged in battle.
Narada said with delight, listen, after due thought I shall tell you of such a person with the rare qualities you named. In the Ikshvaku line is born a prince named Rama. His soul is controlled, his prowess very great, his radiance shines, he has patience and his senses are restrained. He is wise, knows morality, speaks sweetly, is full of grace and destroys his foes. He stays engaged in the good of his subjects and increases the joy of Kausalya.
Rama has broad shoulders, long arms reaching to his knees, a conch-like neck, full jaws, a broad chest and plump shoulder-blades. His head is beautiful, his forehead graceful, his limbs symmetrical, his hue cool and his eyes large. He bears auspicious marks, firmness in promise, purity of body and mind, humility toward elders and self-knowledge. He is skilled in archery, his memory is sharp and he swiftly discerns the truth.
Like Prajapati he protects all, he knows the Vedas and Vedangas, is skilled in every learning, looks equally upon all, and good people come to him as rivers come to the ocean. In gravity he is like the sea, in patience like the Himalaya, in prowess like Vishnu and in beauty like the moon. In anger he is like the fire of dissolution, in forgiveness like the earth, in giving like Kubera and in truth like Dharma himself.
King Dasharatha, who desired the good of his subjects, lovingly prepared to make his virtuous eldest son Rama the heir. Rama's prowess was unfailing and he was devoted to the welfare of the people. Then Queen Kaikeyi asked for the two boons promised earlier. She asked forest exile for Rama and the coronation for Bharata. Bound by the duty of his promise, Dasharatha sent his dear son Rama to the forest, and by his father's order Rama set out to satisfy Kaikeyi.
On the eve of going to the forest, Sumitra's son Lakshmana joined Rama with humility and brotherly love. As Rohini follows the moon, so Sita of Janaka's line followed Rama. She was like divine power, dear to Rama as life, engaged in good deeds, filled with auspicious marks and foremost among wives. Father Dasharatha and the citizens followed far behind. Then at Sringaverapura on the Ganga's bank, Rama met the virtuous dear Nishada king Guha and sent the charioteer back.
Going from one forest to another, crossing many wide rivers, they reached Chitrakuta by Bharadwaja's guidance. There they built a lovely dwelling and lived as they wished, passing days in joy like gods and Gandharvas. Meanwhile, sunk in grief for his son, King Dasharatha wept and went to heaven. After him, though the Brahmanas led by Vashishtha pressed him, Bharata refused the kingdom and set out for the forest to propitiate revered Rama.
Bharata reached Rama and prayed with respect, but Rama said, you who know duty are also king. Because of his father's command Rama did not desire the kingdom and gave his sandals to Bharata and sent him back. Bharata touched Rama's feet and began to rule from Nandigrama hoping for his return. After he left, seeing the crowd of citizens, Rama entered the Dandaka forest. There lotus-eyed Rama killed Viradha and saw Sharabhanga, Sutikshna, Agastya and Agastya's brother.
At Agastya's word Rama gladly took Indra's bow, inexhaustible arrows, sword and quiver. While dwelling in Dandaka the sages came together seeking the destruction of the Rakshasas. Before those ascetics, glowing like fire, Rama vowed to kill the Rakshasas in battle. There Shurpanakha of Janasthana, who could change form at will, was disfigured. At her instigation came Khara, Trishira and Dushana, whom Rama killed with their followers, fourteen thousand Rakshasas were slain in that battle.
When his kinsmen were killed, Ravana, maddened by anger, went to seek Maricha's help. Maricha strongly dissuaded him, do not make enmity with that strong one, spare me, but driven by fate Ravana ignored his words and reached the hermitage with Maricha. Through illusion Maricha drew Rama and Lakshmana far away, then Ravana killed Jatayu and abducted Sita. Seeing the vulture dead and learning that Mithila's princess was taken, Rama wept in grief.
Filled with sorrow Rama cremated Jatayu and while searching for Sita met the dreadful and deformed Rakshasa Kabandha. The mighty-armed one killed him and burned his body, whereupon he went to heaven. That Rakshasa said to Rama, O prince of Raghu, go to the ascetic Shabari who knows morality. Rama the destroyer of foes went to Shabari, Shabari honored him greatly, then on the bank of the Pampa river Rama met Hanuman.
On Hanuman's advice Rama met Sugriva and told him all about Sita. Hearing it, Sugriva was pleased and made friendship with Rama before fire. Then the monkey king sadly told of his enmity with Vali and Rama vowed to kill Vali. Sugriva described Vali's strength and expressed fear, so to convince him he showed the huge corpse of Dundubhi like a hill. Smiling, mighty Rama flung it a full ninety miles away with his toe.
Then with a single strong arrow Rama pierced seven palm trees, a hill and down to the sixth nether world, which fully convinced Sugriva. Pleased, the mighty monkey went with Rama toward the Kishkindha cave. There golden-hued Sugriva roared loudly. At that roar Bali, lord of monkeys, with Tara's permission came forward for battle. Then Rama killed Bali on the spot with a single arrow and at Sugriva's request gave him the kingdom.
Having become king, Sugriva summoned all the monkeys and sent them in all directions to search for Janaki. On Sampati the vulture's advice mighty Hanuman leapt over the hundred-yojana salt sea. In Ravana's Lanka he found Sita sunk in worry in the Ashoka grove. Showing the token he told of Rama and Sugriva's friendship, comforted Videha's daughter and broke the palace gate. Then after killing five generals, seven ministers' sons and the hero Aksha, he was bound by Indrajit's weapon.
Since by the grandsire's boon he was free, Hanuman forgave the Rakshasas who were leading him away. Then sparing only Mithila's daughter's place, he burned all Lanka and returned to give the glad news. Hanuman of boundless spirit circled great-souled Rama and said, I have truly seen Sita. Then with Sugriva Rama went to the shore of the vast ocean and troubled the deep with sun-like arrows. Then the ocean, lord of rivers, appeared.
On the ocean's advice Nala built a bridge over the sea. By that bridge Rama went to Lanka and killed Ravana in battle. On regaining Sita, Rama felt shame and spoke harsh words to Sita before all. Unable to bear it, Sita entered the blazing fire. Then Agni assured Rama of Sita's sinlessness, which greatly pleased Rama and the gods honored him. The three worlds with the moving and unmoving, sages and gods rejoiced. Then Rama gave Lanka's throne to Vibhishana, foremost among Rakshasas.
Obtaining a boon from the gods Rama revived the monkeys fallen in battle and set out for Ayodhya with friends on the Pushpaka chariot. Reaching Bharadwaja's hermitage, Rama true in prowess sent Hanuman to Bharata. Recalling past events, Rama went with Sugriva on the Pushpaka to Nandigrama. There sinless Rama removed his matted locks with his brothers, regained Sita and took back the kingdom. Dasharatha's son Rama, lord of Ayodhya, began to guard his happy subjects like a father.
In his reign his subjects will find happiness and contentment, stay healthy, grow in righteousness and be free from mental unrest, disease and fear of famine. None will see his son die, women will remain ever chaste and never face widowhood. There will be no fear of fire, none will drown in water, no danger from wind, no fever will trouble, no fear of hunger or thieves. Towns and villages will be full of grain and wealth and all will live in joy as in the golden age.
Rama will perform a hundred horse sacrifices with countless gold, give ayutas and kotis of cows to learned men by rite and grant boundless wealth to famed Brahmanas. He will found a hundred royal lines and set the four castes to their own duties. After ruling for ten thousand and a hundred years Rama will go to Brahma's world. Whoever reads this holy, sin-destroying, merit-giving story of Rama as equal to the Veda is freed from all sin.
The man who reads this holy life-lengthening Ramayana is honored in heaven after death with his sons, grandsons and kinsmen. If a Brahmana reads it he attains excellence in speech, if a Kshatriya reads it he attains lordship over land, if a Vaishya reads it he attains abundant wealth in trade, and if a Shudra reads it he attains greatness.
Why does Valmiki ask Narada who the complete man is at this time before beginning the tale, instead of simply starting Rama's story? I see this not as curiosity but as method — first forge the criterion, then seek the person. Valmiki lists qualities — gratitude, truth, firmness in vow, care for the subjects' happiness — because without a standard a story remains mere entertainment. The modern mind makes the same mistake, we search for heroes in stories but never ask what kind of human we must become. Narada's answer that such a person exists gives hope that the ideal can be history, not only sky. This week before watching any inspiring story, write on paper three qualities you want to make your life's measure, then test the story against that same measure.
Why does Narada string bodily marks like a conch-like neck and knee-long arms in the same garland as moral virtues like truth, forgiveness and compassion? In my view this is not body-worship but philosophy — in India the body is character's certificate. A broad chest is not only strength but readiness to carry responsibility, large eyes are not only beauty but practice of equal vision, a cool hue signals victory over agitation. Modern psychology also says patience shows in shoulder tension and anger in the clenching of the jaw. Rama's portrait teaches that virtues are not abstract slogans, they descend into daily flesh. This week once a day, not through mirror or camera, observe your shoulders, breath and movement of eyes and ask which quality your body is revealing today.
Why does Rama accept an unjust exile to protect Dasharatha's word, and why does Bharata, offered an unsought kingdom, leave with the sandals for Nandigrama? I feel the deeper issue here is trust, not obedience. If Dasharatha's boon broke, the institution of the king's word would break, so Rama sacrifices his right to save collective faith. Bharata reverses the same logic — to accept a throne gained by maneuver would break the order a second time. Both brothers treat power not as prasad but as trust, so one ripens in the forest and the other waits in a village. Against modern hunger for power, this is a revolutionary psychology. This week where some gain, credit or post is coming to you without effort, pause for a day and ask whether accepting it will strengthen trust in the system.
Why must Ayodhya's prince make a fire-witnessed friendship with forest-dwellers like Sugriva and Hanuman to win over Ravana? In my reading this is the Ramayana's most modern signal — when the center breaks, the periphery saves. The palace's intrigue exiled Rama, but the forest gave him search, army and flight. Hanuman's leap over a hundred yojanas to see Sita in the Ashoka grove shows the epic wins not by valor alone but by relationships. The hero does not win alone, he wins by joining a different world. Greatness lies not in height but in connection. This week for any stuck problem, seek advice or help from one person outside your rank and familiar circle.
Why does Rama speak harsh words before all after winning Sita back, and why does the tale then imagine a Ramarajya with no untimely death or fear? I think here is the double trial of the ideal. The fire ordeal shows public suspicion wounds even private love — Rama pays the price of becoming the man of limits, and Sita by entering fire declares her autonomy. The Ramarajya described after is not a report but a vow — a map of a society free from fear, hunger and theft. It warns us that even an ideal person can err, so an ideal order is needed. This week when you doubt a loved one, choose proof and dialogue before accusation, and make one concrete rule for fearlessness in your home or team.
