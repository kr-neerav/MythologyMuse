Previously, we saw how Valmiki asked Narada about the perfectly righteous man, and Narada narrated Rama's brief story before returning to his own realm. Afterwards Valmiki sank deep into meditation, and once Rama had gained the kingdom, he composed the entire Ramayana in twenty-four thousand verses. From today we will hear that great dynasty's tale right from the beginning, the dynasty in which Shri Rama was born.
This great tale of the Ramayana is about those victorious kings whose line begins with Prajapati and whose foundation was laid by Ikshvaku. In this very line was born Sagara, who dug the ocean with his mighty strength, and whose sixty thousand sons followed behind him when he marched out. Now we will chant the full history of that dynasty from the start, so listen with a mind free of ill will, for this story grants merit, profit and joy.
On the banks of the Sarayu river lay a large and flourishing land called Kosala, where corn ripened in the fields and homes overflowed with grain and wealth. Its people lived a calm and happy life, lacking nothing they needed. The capital of this land was Ayodhya, founded by Manu himself, the foremost among men. Because it was built by Manu, this city's fame had spread across the whole world.
This beautiful and mighty city stretched twelve yojanas in length and ten yojanas in breadth. Outside, broad and straight roads were laid out in perfect order. Blown flower petals lay scattered on them and water was sprinkled every day. The clean, wide highways shone from afar. In this prosperous city lived King Dasharatha, just as Indra, the lord of gods, dwells in heaven.
Ayodhya had strong doors and grand gateways, and rows of neatly arranged shops brought every street to life. The city held every kind of weapon and tool, and skilled artisans of every craft lived here. Eulogists who sang praises and genealogists who recited lineages were present in great numbers. Flags fluttering on lofty mansions could be seen from far away, and hundreds of Shataghni weapons guarded its safety.
The city had theatres for women, green gardens and dense mango groves that made it truly lovely. A strong wall ran all around it, with a deep moat dug outside. Because of this, neither friend nor foe could enter without permission. The roads were always crowded with elephants, horses, cows, camels and donkeys. The markets echoed with neighbouring kings come to pay tribute and merchants arrived from distant lands.
Mountain-like palaces studded with gems glittered brightly, and lovely pleasure grounds for women lay on every side, so Ayodhya looked like Indra's Amaravati. Courtesans adorned with burnished gold, every kind of jewel, and royal mansions added to its splendour. Paddy and rice grew in abundance here, and the water was as sweet as sugarcane juice. The streets always resounded with the sound of dundubhis, mridangas, veenas and panavas.
It was the most beautiful place on earth, like a celestial chariot gained in heaven by perfected sages through austerities, and it was filled with the finest of people. King Dasharatha had filled the city with thousands of Maharatha warriors whose hands were swift as lightning. They could slay raging lions, tigers and boars roaming the forest, yet they would never loose an arrow at one who was lost, helpless, hiding or fleeing.
Ayodhya abounded most of all in excellent Brahmanas who lit the sacrificial fire every day. They were learned in the Vedas and Vedangas, ever firm in truth, and gave away gifts by the thousands. These high-souled scholars shone like great ascetics. Filled with such righteous people, Ayodhya was truly a heaven-like city where virtue and prosperity flourished together.
Question: Why does Valmiki sing of bygone kings like Prajapati, Ikshvaku and Sagara before Rama is even born? Reflection: In my view this genealogy is not nostalgia, it is psychology — we imagine a person as a lonely shining star, but Valmiki shows him as the last ghat of a river. Sagara dug the ocean with sixty thousand sons, meaning one generation reshapes the map of nature so the next can swim in it. Rama's restraint did not appear suddenly, it is the interest earned on thousands of years of discipline. Modern culture worships the self-made hero, so we become rootless and break. Takeaway: This week write down your three inheritances — which value came from which ancestor or teacher — and resolve to carry one of them forward.
Question: Why is the listener asked to abandon ill will before the story even begins? Reflection: For me this warning protects not the story but the listener, it is a therapy. Valmiki says this tale grants merit, profit and joy, but a mind full of envy compares instead of receiving. Hearing Ayodhya described, the first reaction can be jealousy — so much happiness, so much perfection! The envious listener starts counting what he lacks instead of learning. Modern scroll-culture teaches the same — to burn seeing others prosper, not to understand. So to be free of envy is not spiritual etiquette, it is a technique of learning. Takeaway: This week whenever sourness rises seeing another's success, pause and write — what can I learn from this — and answer it in one sentence.
Question: Why does a scripture measure Ayodhya's spirituality in flower-strewn roads and water sweet as sugarcane? Reflection: I feel Valmiki is breaking our sacred-versus-poor divide. In Kosala corn ripens, shops are adorned, highways are sprinkled with water — this is not indulgence, it is proof of dharma. Where there is a founder like Manu and a guardian like Dasharatha, even nature cooperates and water turns sweet. A clean road and sweet water are by-products of ethics, not decoration. Today we treat development as the enemy of soul, so we build either malls or ashrams. Ayodhya says order itself is worship. Takeaway: This week choose ten feet outside your home — clean it daily, sprinkle water, plant one sapling — and run a small Ayodhya experiment.
Question: What are Shataghni weapons, theatres for women and courtesans doing in the same ideal city? Reflection: In my reading Ayodhya defines purity not by prohibition but by balance. Here there are hundreds of Shataghnis for security, yet also gardens, mango groves and pleasure grounds for women for joy. Amid gem-studded palaces and the echo of veena and mridanga, Veda-knowing Brahmanas give gifts by the thousands — meaning knowledge is not miserly, it is generous. Courtesans are called adornment, not stigma, because desire is not suppressed but refined. The modern city becomes either fortress or bazaar, Ayodhya binds both in dharma. Takeaway: This week draw your daily-life triangle — security, joy, wisdom — and invest one solid hour in whichever corner lies empty.
Question: Why are Maharathas who can slay lions praised for not shooting the hidden and the fleeing? Reflection: To me this is Ayodhya's sharpest definition — civilization is measured not by capability but by restraint. Dasharatha's warriors have lightning-swift hands, they can kill raging beasts of the forest, yet they will not touch the helpless, the frightened or the one who turns his back. That is, heroism lies not in striking but in withholding the strike. Wall and deep moat make the city impregnable from outside, but this moral moat makes it great from inside. Today power means crushing the moment you get a chance — from trolling to warfare. Ayodhya says to spare whom you could slay is true Rajdharma. Takeaway: Once a day pause and notice — where you could win by argument, rank or sarcasm — deliberately hold back your arrow and let the other leave safely.
